I make a living writing things for other people to read, so the irony of this article isn’t lost on me.

We all have our professional frustrations, but one of my particular bugbears is being told that ‘nobody reads anymore’. It’s usually followed by some well-meaning advice about making everything shorter, using more visuals or turning whatever I’ve written into a video.

But before businesses rethink how they invest in content, I think it’s worth asking whether people really have lost their appetite for reading or whether we’re simply producing a lot of stuff that nobody particularly wants to read.

## Does anybody read anymore?

Back in 1997, research by Nielsen Norman Group found that 79% of its test users scanned web pages and only 16% read them word for word. This was before Google, smartphones, social media and most of the things we now routinely blame for the demise of our attention spans.

The difference is that we now have a lot more content to sift through.

I can spend an embarrassing amount of time reading about something completely frivolous simply because it piques my interest. Yet put a 300-word corporate announcement in front of me and my attention span is apparently that of a goldfish. So maybe our attention spans haven’t become shorter; they’ve just had to become more selective.

## A lot of words, not a lot to say

In 2021, research by Edelman and LinkedIn found that 71% of business decision-makers gained useful insights from half or less of the thought leadership they consumed. This was before ChatGPT gave us the ability to produce quite plausible-sounding thought leadership on an industrial scale without necessarily having had a thought in the first place.

I sometimes wonder how much business content starts with somebody having something interesting to say and how much starts with an empty cell in a marketing calendar.

I’m not knocking content calendars. I spend a fair amount of time populating them. But somewhere along the way, we seem to have confused being prolific with being interesting. The fact that we haven’t published anything this week might give us a reason to write something, but it hardly gives anybody else a reason to read it.

And if the only thing distinguishing your article from those of your five closest competitors is the company logo at the top, what exactly are you hoping to achieve?

## How short is short enough?

Chartbeat’s analysis of millions of online articles found that average engaged time increased with article length up to around 2,000 words.

That’s not an argument for subjecting your customers to a 2,000-word treatise on your latest product upgrade, but it does challenge the commonly held assumption that shorter is better.

There’s a difference between removing unnecessary words and removing the substance of an argument. Not every interesting idea lends itself to five bullet points and a graphic.

## Is anybody paying attention?

The 2026 Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report threw up an interesting finding. While 84% of decision-makers had used AI to summarise thought leadership during vendor evaluation, 82% believed thought leadership was becoming more valuable as AI became a common source of business information.

So even if people are outsourcing some of their reading, the quality of the thinking behind our content is arguably becoming more important.

The same research found that 76% of decision-makers felt that thought leadership discussed within their buying group helped them assess whether a supplier understood their business needs.

As marketers, we’ve become attached to the things we can count. But there’s no particularly useful column in my monthly analytics spreadsheet for ‘prompted an interesting conversation at a prospective client’s management meeting’.

Before you consign your latest article to the ‘well, that was a waste of time’ pile because it attracted three likes on LinkedIn, two of which were from your colleagues, consider what might be happening beyond your analytics dashboard.

## In defence of the written word

I enjoy a good podcast, appreciate a clever graphic and am quite happy to watch a video, although preferably not one of somebody reading the contents of a PowerPoint slide.

But there’s something I particularly value about reading. I can engage with an idea at my own pace, question an argument or stop altogether because it’s prompted a more interesting train of thought. And while I can now get an explanation of pretty much anything in seconds, I’m still interested in what somebody who knows their subject thinks about it, particularly if they have a different take on it from everybody else.

If your business sells expertise, your experience, knowledge and perspective are some of your most valuable marketing assets, but only if you can communicate them in a way that other people find interesting and useful.

So before asking whether an article is too long, whether it would work better as a video or whether you need to publish more frequently, there’s a more fundamental question worth asking:

If I didn’t work for this company, would I bother reading it?

And if you’ve made it this far, thank you for somewhat undermining the premise of this article.
