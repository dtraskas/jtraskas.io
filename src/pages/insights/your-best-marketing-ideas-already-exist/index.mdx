Specialist consulting firms rarely lack ideas. The challenge is recognising
which ones are worth developing and creating a reliable way to bring them into
the firm’s marketing.

Useful insights emerge all the time. They come up in client conversations,
discussions between colleagues, proposals, presentations and the questions
consultants find themselves answering repeatedly. But much of that knowledge
stays where it started because the people with the most valuable perspectives
are usually focused on delivering client work, building relationships and
winning new business. They may intend to write an article or share their
thinking more widely, but there’s always something more pressing to do.

As a result, the firm’s marketing doesn’t always reflect the depth of its
experience and expertise. Content appears sporadically, social posts become
increasingly difficult to generate, and good ideas are forgotten as attention
moves to the next client priority.

## Make it easy for people to contribute

Asking a busy consultant to write an article is rarely the best place to start.
It gives them another task and usually leaves them staring at a blank page when
they could be doing something chargeable.

A focused conversation is often far more productive. In 30 minutes, a subject
matter expert might explain:

- A problem they’re seeing more frequently
- A common misconception among clients
- Why a familiar approach isn’t delivering the expected result
- What businesses should be doing differently
- A recent conversation that changed their thinking

The conversation doesn’t need to produce a perfectly formed argument. Its
purpose is to uncover the substance: what the expert has noticed, why it matters
and whether there’s something useful to say about it.

This approach makes it easier to involve people who are unlikely to volunteer
ideas for marketing. Some of the strongest perspectives may come from those
closest to delivery rather than the people who are already comfortable posting
on LinkedIn.

## Not every good idea is a marketing idea

Capturing knowledge is only the first step. Not every interesting conversation
needs to become an article, and not every technical development will matter to
clients.

Someone needs to apply a degree of commercial judgement.

Does the idea connect with a problem clients are facing? Does it reinforce what
the firm wants to be known for? Can the business say something useful or
distinctive about it? Is it relevant now, or would it be more valuable later?

These questions help separate genuine thought leadership from content produced
simply because the marketing calendar needs filling.

They also make it possible to spot connections between ideas. A question raised
by one client may relate to a challenge discussed by another team, a service the
firm is developing or a wider change taking place in the market. Individually,
those observations might lead to a single post. Together, they could form the
basis of a much stronger theme.

## Develop themes, not isolated pieces of content

A common mistake is to treat every article, post or case study as a separate
task. Something is published, the box is ticked and the search for the next idea
begins.

A more useful approach is to identify the themes that matter to the business and
develop them over time.

An initial observation might lead to a short post that tests whether the subject
resonates. Responses from clients and colleagues can add to the thinking. The
idea might then be explored in greater depth through an article, connected to
relevant client work or used to support a conversation with a prospect.

This isn’t about repeating the same message; it’s about building a recognisable
point of view and giving useful ideas enough time to gain traction.

Over time, the firm develops a connected body of work around the issues it
understands best. Its marketing begins to reflect what people inside the
business are actually seeing, learning and discussing, rather than operating as
a separate activity.

## Consistency needs ownership

Consulting firms don’t necessarily need a large internal marketing team to make
this work. But someone does need to own the process.

That means knowing where ideas are likely to emerge, creating opportunities to
capture them and making it easy for experts to contribute. It requires someone
to decide which ideas support the firm’s priorities, shape them for the right
audience and ensure promising themes don’t disappear after a single post. The
finished content also needs to be structured around the questions clients are
asking and the language they use, so that both people and search tools can
quickly understand its relevance.

AI can make parts of the process faster, particularly when organising source
material or exploring how an idea might be structured. But it can’t decide which
conversations matter to the business or provide the experience behind them. That
still requires human judgement.

Before searching for the next marketing idea, it may be worth paying closer
attention to the conversations already happening inside your firm.

The raw material is probably there. The real opportunity lies in knowing what to
do with it.
