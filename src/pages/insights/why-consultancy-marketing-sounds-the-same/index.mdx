Consulting is an intensely competitive sector. Firms invest time and money in
refining their propositions, redesigning websites, producing content and
building the profiles of their people. They think carefully about how they want
to be seen and why a prospective client should choose them.

Yet look across the websites and LinkedIn pages of consulting firms in almost
any specialism and the same phrases appear again and again.

Transformation. Tailored solutions. Deep expertise. Trusted partnerships.
Measurable results.

None of these claims is necessarily inaccurate. Most consultancies genuinely do
tailor their work, employ knowledgeable people and care about delivering
results. The problem is that these statements could apply to almost anyone.

When so much work has gone into building a distinctive firm, it’s frustrating
when the marketing doesn’t communicate what makes it different.

## Standing out requires more than looking different

A strong visual identity can make a consultancy more recognisable. A
well-designed website creates an important first impression, while consistent
colours, photography and graphics help build familiarity over time.

But design can only do part of the job. If the message underneath it relies on
the same broad claims as everyone else, prospective clients are still left
trying to work out why this particular firm is right for them.

The temptation is to solve the problem by searching for a more original tagline
or a cleverer way to describe the same capabilities. Clearer language certainly
helps, but differentiation isn’t simply a writing exercise. The words need to be
based on something real.

That might be a particular understanding of a client problem, an approach shaped
by years of delivery experience or a clear belief about why familiar solutions
don’t always work. Without that substance, even the most polished copy will
struggle to make the firm memorable.

## Capabilities aren’t the same as differentiation

Most consulting firms can explain what they do. Their websites list services,
sectors, methodologies and credentials, usually supported by statements about
the outcomes they deliver.

This information has a place. A prospective client needs to know whether a firm
has relevant experience and can provide the support they need. But a list of
capabilities doesn’t necessarily explain why they should choose one qualified
firm over another.

Differentiation is more likely to emerge from how a consultancy sees and
approaches a problem:

- What does it believe clients regularly misunderstand?
- Where do conventional approaches fall short?
- What patterns has its team observed across different engagements?
- What does it do differently, and why?
- What has it learned that could help clients make better decisions?

These questions begin to reveal a point of view rather than another description
of services.

For example, saying that a firm delivers “tailored transformation solutions”
tells us very little. Explaining why transformation programmes often lose
momentum after the initial strategy phase, and what the firm does to prevent
that, gives a prospective client something much more useful to consider.

The second message may not appeal to everyone, and that’s partly what makes it
valuable.

## Why distinctive thinking gets diluted

Consultancies don’t set out to sound generic. The language often becomes broader
as the firm tries to accommodate multiple services, sectors and internal
perspectives.

Everyone needs to recognise their part of the business in the message. Important
nuances are added, claims are softened and anything that might narrow the
audience is removed. By the time the wording has passed through several rounds
of review, it may be accurate and widely acceptable, but much of its original
character has disappeared.

There can also be a reluctance to take a clear position. A broad promise can
feel safer than saying what the firm believes, particularly when leaders worry
that a more focused message could exclude potential clients.

But trying to speak to everyone creates a different risk. The people the firm
most wants to reach may not recognise themselves or their problems in the
message at all.

A distinctive point of view doesn’t require a provocative opinion on every
industry issue. Nor does it mean making sweeping claims that can’t be supported.
It requires the company to be specific about the problems it really understands,
the lessons it has learned and the principles that shape its work.

## AI could make the problem worse

AI can help consulting firms produce content more quickly. But speed alone isn’t
an advantage if the output sounds like everything else in the market.

Given a broad prompt, AI is very good at producing polished consultancy
language. In seconds, it can generate an article about navigating complexity,
embracing transformation or unlocking sustainable value. The result may be
perfectly readable, but it rarely gives the audience a reason to remember who
wrote it.

As more firms use the same tools to discuss the same subjects, there’s a real
risk of producing a greater volume of increasingly similar content.

AI is far more useful when it has distinctive thinking to work with. It can
support research, test an argument, identify gaps, suggest a structure and help
refine the writing. What it can’t do is provide the experience that sits behind
the firm’s point of view.

The differentiator isn’t the ability to produce more content. It’s having
something meaningful to say and using AI intelligently to help communicate it.

## Look for the ideas you return to

A firm’s strongest positioning often already exists in fragments. It can be
heard in the points its leaders make repeatedly, the advice consultants give
clients and the principles that influence how the work is delivered.

It may appear in an explanation of why the firm declined to follow a
conventional approach, a lesson from a difficult engagement or a question that
consistently changes the direction of a client conversation.

The task is to identify these threads and decide which ones genuinely matter to
the market. Once articulated clearly, they can shape far more than an occasional
article. They can strengthen the website, inform proposals and presentations,
give social content greater consistency and help people across the firm explain
its value in a more compelling way.

Consistency matters because differentiation isn’t created by a single bold
statement. It develops when clients encounter the same recognisable thinking
across different channels and conversations, supported by evidence and expressed
in language that sounds like the firm rather than the rest of the sector.

When so many consultancies are competing for attention, clearer language will
only take you so far. Real differentiation begins with being clear about what
your firm knows, believes and does differently, and being willing to say it.
